Read the pronunciation

Pinyin → Mandarin

水 “water” is shuǐ. The letters describe the syllable; the mark above the vowel gives its tone.

Jyutping → Cantonese

水 “water” is seoi2. The letters describe the Cantonese pronunciation; the final digit gives its tone.

These are pronunciation systems, not translations. Simplified and traditional characters are writing choices; either can be paired with Mandarin or Cantonese pronunciation.

A tone is the pitch pattern within a syllable. It can help distinguish words, so learn it together with the letters. Higher pitch does not mean louder, and you do not need a musical singing voice.

Read your first Pinyin syllable

Take hǎo (好, “good”): h is the beginning sound, ao is the ending, and ǎ marks tone 3. You may also see it typed as hao3.

Pinyin letters have their own sound values. In particular, q, x, and zh are not read as English spellings. Learn them from recordings rather than guessing. The vowel ü, as in nǚ (女), is different from u; its dots are omitted after j, q, x and in spellings beginning with y.

Start with the classic mā · má · mǎ · mà comparison. The letters stay the same, but each tone points to a different word. Read each pitch line from left to right; higher means higher in your comfortable speaking range. These are sketches of pitch, not exact musical notes.

Neutral tone: an unmarked syllable is often lighter and shorter, as with de in 我的 (wǒ de, “my/mine”). Its pitch depends on the surrounding speech. Numbered learning materials sometimes use 0 or 5 for it.

Why speech does not always match the isolated diagram

  • Third tone usually stays low before another syllable. Do not force a large down-and-up movement into every word.
  • Two third tones together: in 你好, the first syllable is pronounced with a rising tone. You will commonly still see the underlying spelling nǐ hǎo; ní hǎo can help show how it sounds.
  • A few common words change tone too. 不 is normally bù, but before another fourth tone it becomes bú, as in 不是 (bú shì, “is not”). 一 (yī) also changes in many combinations. Learn those changes through whole phrases.

For more detail: MIT’s Mandarin tone notes and the University of Texas Pinyin guide.

Read your first Jyutping syllable

In nei5 (你, “you”), n begins the syllable, ei completes the sound, and 5 gives the tone. The number is not pronounced aloud. Jyutping uses six tone numbers and does not add Pinyin-style tone marks.

A few spelling clues: j is a y-like beginning sound, aa and a represent different vowels, and ng can begin a syllable or stand on its own, as in ng5 (五, “five”). These spellings belong to Cantonese: do not read them using Pinyin rules.

Six tone numbers

Notice that several tones are level. Listen for their relative height as well as whether the voice moves.

For a useful contrast, compare 買 maai5 (“buy”) with 賣 maai6 (“sell”). Keep the letters the same and change the pitch pattern.

Why do some books say nine tones? Syllables ending in -p, -t, or -k stop abruptly. Traditional descriptions count these “entering” tones separately. Jyutping writes them with 1, 3, or 6, so you still learn six tone numbers. Do not add a vowel after the final stop.

A Jyutping number is not a Pinyin number. Mandarin tone 3 is low/dipping; Cantonese tone 3 is mid-level. Keep the two systems separate when practising.

The Linguistic Society of Hong Kong’s Jyutping scheme explains the sounds and tone categories. The Chinese University of Hong Kong’s tone lesson provides listening examples.

Same syllable, different tone, different meaning

Some words look almost identical in romanization until you notice the tone. Think of them as sound-alikes to keep apart: saying “sell” when you mean “buy” changes your message.

Listen to each word on its own, imitate its pitch, then watch or write the character. The different characters help you keep the meanings separate. These are tone contrasts between different words; context still matters in a real conversation.

  2. Listen before reading aloud. Repeat the whole syllable, then trace its pitch with your hand. Keep your voice comfortable.
  3. Record and compare. Check the starting height and direction. Aim for a clear contrast, not a dramatic performance.
  4. Put it into a word or phrase. Listen again in context; a dictionary reading cannot show every change in connected speech.
